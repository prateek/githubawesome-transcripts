## Backstory

[0:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGypNxhuphI&t=39)

But first, a bit of backstory. This channel, GitHub Awesome, started last October, and the reason I started it wasn't passion or a brilliant strategy or a content gap I spotted in the market. I lost my job. So, I did what a lot of developers do when they suddenly have time on their hands. I started building something. in this case, videos. I figured I already spend time browsing GitHub's trending repos every day. Why not document that? Why not make it useful for other people, too? Turns out, people agreed it was useful. Since then, I've published around 80 long form videos and roughly 130 shorts. My schedule has stayed pretty consistent.

One short per day and two long videos per week. One covering weekly trending repos, one covering daily trending repos. Are they different? Honestly, not that much. It's all trending GitHub content. I do too, mainly because the volume of great open- source projects never stops and it keeps me from getting lazy.

## Numbers

[1:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGypNxhuphI&t=100)

Okay, the numbers. 2 million total views. Cash earned from YouTube ads, approximately $500. That's it. That's the honest answer. Now, to be fair, that $500 is purely AdSense revenue. On top of that, I have 10 channel members, which adds a small amount monthly. I want to call those 10 people out specifically because that kind of direct support means far more than the dollar amount suggests. And three people have bought me a coffee. You know who you are. Thank you. Since crossing certain follower thresholds, my inbox has been filling up with sponsorship offers.

Almost all of them AI related tools or SAS products. I've turned down most of them. And I want to explain why this channel exists because I want to give developers real unfiltered signal about what's happening in open source. The moment I start accepting money to promote pro products I haven't vetted, I compromise that I'm not doing it. At least not without a standard I'm comfortable with.

## Why

[2:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGypNxhuphI&t=162)

Why do I keep going if the money is this low? Two reasons. First, I genuinely enjoy this. Tracking GitHub trends, discovering new tools before they blow up, synthesizing them into something a developer can understand in minutes. This is the work. It's exhausting, but it's also the most engaged I've felt professionally in a long time. It tests your discipline more than any 9 to5 ever did. But if the underlying work is something you actually care about, you find a way to keep going. Second, you, the people who watch seriously, comment thoughtfully and push back when I get something wrong. You're why the daily schedule still happens.

## Comment

[3:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGypNxhuphI&t=203)

Now, that comment, the most common thing people write in my comments is some version of, "This is just an AI generated channel. There's no real person here." I'll be direct. That's frustrating to read. Not because I'm offended, but because it means people are watching something I spent real time on and walking away thinking no human was involved. So, let me walk you through what actually goes into each video. Every day, I manually browse GitHub trending to find projects worth covering. I record all the on-screen footage personally. I use AI for the voice over. Yes, that part is AI assisted. And then I edit everything together in Cap Cut. Is AI involved?

Yes, in one specific step. But the thinking, the curation, the judgment about what matters and why, that's mine. No tool automates that. No tool can tell you why a 3we old Rust CLI deserves your attention over a trending JavaScript repo with 10,000 stars. That's a judgment call. That takes a person. Artificial intelligence is a tool I use. It's not the creator.
